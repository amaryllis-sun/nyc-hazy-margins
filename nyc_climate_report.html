Is the air quality in these cities actually growing worse, or is it our perception?

My name is Amaryllis and I've lived in NYC my whole life. Recently, every summer seems to be hotter than the last. Weather reports seem to constantly record spikes up to 100 degrees, while nearby wildfires bring smoke and other pollutants to the city, making the sky hazier and New Yorkers concerned. So, I wanted to look into this phenomenon and think beyond face-value, using actual data to prove (or challenge) my hypotheses. I built a PostgreSQL database from public EPA and NOAA data to support my findings, and in turn, to compare NYC against four other major U.S. cities. Turns out, the answer is a bit of both.

Method

Breaking Down the Database

A PostgreSQL database built from two federal sources — the EPA's Air Quality System API (daily PM2.5 and AQI, by monitoring station) and NOAA's Climate Data Online API (daily temperature) — covering 2015 through 2025 across five U.S. cities, plus a full five-borough breakdown for New York City. I wanted consistent data that was frequently updated so that my analysis of summers went further than just the ones I remembered in recent years, hence why a decade timeline was chosen. These two sources provided insight regarding pollutants that New Yorkers from all five boroughs might not necessarily know of, especially from just checking the weather app. Furthermore, a third dataset, NYC's Heat Vulnerability Index, adds a socioeconomic layer by ZIP code. We used the Census Bureau's geocoder to match each ZIP code to its corresponding station reading. This way, we can see if there exists a trend between vulnerable communities having worse air quality and higher temperatures.

Finding 01

Contrary to popular belief, NYC's air quality doesn't have trends, just spikes

Averaged across Manhattan's EPA monitors, yearly AQI bounces between the high 30s and mid 40s with no apparent upward or downward line. The one year that stands out, 2023, wasn't part of a trend — it was the Canadian wildfire smoke that spread to the NYC area.

Average AQI & PM2.5 by year, Manhattan monitors, 2015–2025
2023 recorded 3 unhealthy-air days (AQI > 100) — more than any other year in the dataset, and the direct signature of the June 2023 wildfire smoke event. 2024 dropped straight back to zero, demonstrating the lack of a clear trend-line.
Finding 02

Hot days are slowly on the rise

The number of 90°F+ days each year is trending upward. A linear fit puts it at roughly +0.5 days per year. But the fit is loose — R² = 0.19, meaning most of the year-to-year variation comes from ordinary weather changes, not a steady climb. To copmare: 2020 had just one hot day while 2025 had fourteen.

Days at 90°F+ per year, Central Park station, with linear trend (slope 0.5, R² 0.19)

Finding 03

NYC isn't the worst of the five cities, LA is.

Houston beats New York on average AQI, and Los Angeles doesn't even hold the highest average. However, LA has more than double the unhealthy-air days of any other city. A decent average can help to hide sequences of bad days.

Finding 04

Manhattan's AQI seems to be the worst, but there isn't a clear relationship between heat vulnerability and AQI

Avg AQI by borough (monitor count in parentheses)
Avg AQI by Heat Vulnerability Index (1=lowest risk, 5=highest)

Manhattan's monitors average the highest AQI (42) while Staten Island/Queens hold the lowest (37), a real but modest gradient. What wasn't clear, though, was an obvious relationship between measured air quality and NYC's Heat Vulnerability Index. The most heat-vulnerable ZIP codes (HVI 5) show average air quality, not the worst.

This is actually a useful result! HVI is determined by median income, green space, and AC access within communities, not necessarily pollution readings. It measures who suffers most when a heat wave arrives (hence the vulnerability aspect of the name), not where the air is dirtiest on paper. Current exposure and ability to survive in case of future exposure are different components of climate risk.
Honest limitations

What This Data Doesn't Say

• NYC's borough comparison rests on 14 EPA monitors total (2 to 4 per borough), not a comprehensive enough grid to call any single ZIP code "worse". It mainly works to spot city-scale patterns.

• Temperature for all five NYC boroughs comes from one station (Central Park) due to its consistent and long-term data. Air quality is borough-specific while heat is currently limited.

• Correlation between hot days and AQI doesn't separate heat itself from general summer trends, as ozone chemistry, wind patterns, and wildfire smoke all tend to occur in the same months.

• Five cities and eleven years is a real dataset, not a complete one. As such, it generalizes patterns instead of forming nationally-specific conclusions.

The SQL, if you're curious

How the Linear Regression Was Calculated

Postgres's built-in regression functions did the trend-line math directly, no third-party stats library import was needed:

WITH yearly_hot_days AS ( SELECT EXTRACT(YEAR FROM d.reading_date) AS year, COUNT(DISTINCT d.reading_date) AS hot_days FROM daily_readings d JOIN weather_readings w ON d.reading_date = w.reading_date WHERE w.max_temp_f >= 90 GROUP BY 1 ) SELECT REGR_SLOPE(hot_days, year) AS days_gained_per_year, REGR_R2(hot_days, year) AS r_squared FROM yearly_hot_days;
